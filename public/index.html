Who I work with

Adults and adolescents. Often people who have recently been diagnosed with ADHD or autism, people living with a psychosocial disability, and people whose daily function has come apart — sometimes for reasons they can name, sometimes not.

What that usually looks like in practice:

  • Routines that hold for a week and then don't
  • Knowing exactly what needs doing and not being able to start
  • Time that disappears without an account of where it went
  • Environments that are quietly exhausting to be in
  • Systems that work for other people and not for you

I see NDIS participants who are plan-managed or self-managed, private clients, and people referred by their GP. If you are not sure whether this is the right kind of help, ask me — I will tell you plainly if it isn't.

What occupational therapy addresses

Occupational therapy is a registered health profession concerned with function — what you actually do across a day, and what makes that harder or easier. It is practical and collaborative rather than interpretive.

Sessions typically cover three connected areas:

Time and task

  • Starting when you can't begin
  • Breaking down multi-step tasks
  • Time awareness and realistic estimates
  • Switching tasks without losing the thread
  • Admin, bills and appointments that pile up

Sensory and regulation

  • Noise, light and visual load at home and work
  • Recognising overload before it arrives
  • Recovery that is planned, not collapse
  • Setting up a space you can actually work in

Rhythm and routine

  • Routines built to survive a bad week
  • Sleep–wake structure
  • Meals, laundry and the domestic baseline
  • Keeping up with work or study

This work sits alongside psychological therapy and medical care. It does not replace either, and I will say so if what you need sits outside what occupational therapy can offer.

What the reasoning rests on

Occupational therapy has a body of theory behind it, and it is reasonable to ask what a practitioner is actually working from. These are the frameworks that shape how I assess and what I suggest.

Person–Environment–Occupation
Performance is the fit between you, the task and the setting. Changing the environment or the task is as legitimate a move as changing you — often more durable.
Model of Human Occupation
What you want to do, what you are in the habit of doing, and what you can currently sustain are three different things. Plans that ignore any one of them tend to fail.
Sensory processing framework
Sensory thresholds differ between people and across a day. The aim is a workable fit between your threshold and the environment you are in, not tolerance training.
CO-OP
A guided strategy-discovery approach — goal, plan, do, check — so the method transfers to problems we never sat down and worked on together.
Measured against your goals
Goals are set in your words at the start and reviewed against the same wording at the end, rather than against a general impression of progress.

Underneath all of it: difficulty with a daily task is treated as information about the fit between a person and their circumstances, not as evidence about character.

How it works

The whole path, so you know what you are agreeing to before you agree to any of it.

  1. You get in touch

    A sentence about what you'd like help with is enough. I reply within [2] business days, and I'll tell you plainly if I think someone else is a better fit.

  2. A short questionnaire

    I email a few questions about a typical day and what you've already tried. Answer what you want to — partial answers are genuinely fine, and they save session time either way.

  3. Initial session

    [60] minutes, in person or by video. We work out what is actually getting in the way, rather than what it looks like from the outside.

  4. Findings and goals

    I set out what I think is happening and why, in plain language. Goals get written in your words, not clinical ones, and they are what we measure against later.

  5. A short block of sessions

    Usually [4–6]. Practical work between sessions, sized to what a bad week can absorb rather than a good one.

  6. Review, and a written summary

    We look at the original goals in their original wording and decide together whether to continue, pause, or stop. You get a short written summary of what we did and what helped.

What the work looks like

A morning that eats the day

Presenting Recently diagnosed with ADHD. Mornings take three hours, so work starts late and runs late, which pushes sleep back again.

A home that is quietly exhausting

Presenting Autistic adult, working from home, describing a constant low-level depletion with no single obvious cause.

What we work on A sensory audit of the spaces used most. Adjusting light, sound and visual load. Building recovery into the day deliberately instead of after collapse.

Systems that keep failing

Presenting Long history of planners, apps and routines that hold for a week and then stop, with the failure read as a character problem.

What we work on Why each system failed, treated as design information rather than fault. Rebuilding one routine at a time around how attention actually behaves.

Common questions

Do I need a referral from my GP?

No. You can contact me directly and book without a referral. A referral is only needed if you are claiming through a Medicare care plan, in which case your GP arranges it. NDIS participants who are plan-managed or self-managed don't need one either.

Can I use NDIS or private health cover?

Yes to both. I see plan-managed and self-managed NDIS participants at the NDIS Pricing Schedule rates. For private health, I issue a receipt with my provider number for you to claim, if your extras cover includes occupational therapy. I'm not able to invoice the NDIA directly, so agency-managed participants can't currently be seen.

Do you offer telehealth?

Yes, by video across New South Wales — and it's a genuine option rather than a fallback. Much of this work is about your actual home and routines, and being in that space during a session is often more useful than describing it from a clinic room.

What happens in the first session?

We work out what is actually getting in the way, rather than what it looks like from the outside. Expect questions about a typical day, where it reliably comes apart, and what you've already tried. You'll leave with a clear picture of what we would work on — and no obligation to book again.

How is this different from psychology or coaching?

Occupational therapy is a registered health profession concerned with function: what you do across a day, and what makes it harder or easier. The work is practical and environmental rather than interpretive — it changes the task, the setting and the routine as much as the strategy. It sits alongside psychological therapy and medical care rather than replacing either.

What if I need to change or cancel a session?

Give me [24 / 48] hours and there's no charge. Inside that window the session is charged in full, because the time can't be reallocated — though I waive it for genuine emergencies, and I don't interrogate what counts. If you're too flat to attend, say so; we can often do a shorter or lighter session instead of cancelling.

I'm not sure occupational therapy is what I need.

That's a reasonable position and worth saying in your first email. Describe the problem rather than the solution and I'll tell you honestly whether this is the right service — including if it isn't, and who might be better placed. There's no cost to asking, and I'd rather redirect you early than take a booking that won't help.
